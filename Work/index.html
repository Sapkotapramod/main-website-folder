How this matches the real solar system

Real positions mode. Positions come from NASA/JPL's published Keplerian orbit elements for the planets (E. M. Standish and J. G. Williams). For any date the page works out each planet's orbit shape and tilt, solves Kepler's equation, and places the planet. Between 1800 and 2050 it uses JPL's high-accuracy set (errors from a few arcseconds for Mercury to about 10 arcminutes for Saturn). For all other dates, up to 3000 BC and 3000 AD, it uses JPL's long-range set, which is accurate to within about half a degree in the worst case.

Newton physics mode. The page takes the real positions and speeds on your chosen date, then moves every planet using only F = G × M × m / (r + h)² between all the bodies. The hollow rings show the real planets. If the filled planets stay on their rings, Newton's law is reproducing the real motion. The gap is shown in the card in kilometres and degrees.

Why planets do not fall into the Sun. They do fall, but they are also moving sideways very fast, so they keep missing it. That endless falling-and-missing is an orbit. Double the distance and the pull becomes four times weaker, which is why Neptune moves slowly and Mercury races.

Limits: the view is a top-down 2D picture of 3D orbits. Earth is the Earth-Moon system's centre (barycentre), so its mass includes the Moon. Dates before 15 October 1582 use the Julian calendar, as in history books. Dates are in dynamical time, which differs from clock time by seconds to minutes in modern times and more in ancient times. Source: NASA JPL Solar System Dynamics. For exact positions, use JPL Horizons.
