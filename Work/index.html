How this matches the real solar system

Real positions. Orbit positions come from NASA/JPL's published Keplerian elements (E. M. Standish and J. G. Williams). Between 1800 and 2050 it uses JPL's high-accuracy table. From 3000 BC to 3000 AD it uses JPL's long-range table, accurate to within about half a degree in the worst case.

Day and night. Each planet's spin axis direction and rotation rate come from the IAU working group values. The page combines the planet's real spin angle with the real direction of the Sun to find where it is noon, where it is night, and where the Sun is straight overhead. For Earth this is accurate to about a minute of time. For other planets the rotation phase is approximate, so it is good for learning but not for exact predictions.

Sunrise on other worlds. A solar day is not the same as one spin. Mercury spins once in 58.6 Earth days but has a 176-day solar day. Venus spins backwards and has a 117-day solar day. Uranus lies on its side, so its poles get decades of continuous daylight and darkness.

Newton mode. Starting from the real positions, only F = G × M × m / (r + h)² between all bodies moves the planets. If the filled planets stay on their rings, Newton's law is reproducing the real motion.

Limits: the main view is a top-down 2D picture of 3D orbits. Earth's position is the Earth-Moon centre of mass. Continents are drawn very coarsely and other planets show simple colours and latitude lines. Gas giants have no solid surface, so their rotation is the internal one.
